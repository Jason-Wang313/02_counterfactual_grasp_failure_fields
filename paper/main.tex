\begin{abstract}
Robot grasp failures are usually stored as terminal labels: a grasp succeeded,
failed, slipped, or had low quality.  Such labels are useful for prediction but
discard the contact-level intervention that would have made the realized grasp
work.  We propose a different object, a \emph{counterfactual grasp failure
field}: for a failed tactile/contact state, the field is the lowest-cost
physically executable edit to contact locations or forces that crosses a
mechanical success boundary.  In a planar two-contact pinch model, this field is
the minimum-norm projection of the realized contact state onto the feasible
wrench interval.  The model yields a small but sharp impossibility result:
paired failures can have identical scalar failure scores while requiring
opposite contact repairs.  A reproducible simulation with 20,000 feasible failed
grasps confirms the consequence: the analytic failure field repairs every case
in one step, while scalar-only repair rules that know failure magnitude but not
contact-field direction succeed at approximately chance.  The evidence is not a
real-robot validation; it is a mechanistic counterexample showing that failure
representation, not only failure prediction, is a central design choice for
tactile grasp repair.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Grasping research has strong tools for deciding whether a grasp is promising:
force-closure tests, wrench-space quality metrics, dense visual affordances, and
learned tactile stability predictors.  These tools answer variants of a scalar
question: will this grasp hold?  But a robot that has already touched the object
needs a different answer.  It needs to know which contact should move, by how
much, and in which direction.

This paper asks whether a grasp failure should be represented as an error label
or as a counterfactual contact object.  We argue for the latter.  A
\emph{counterfactual grasp failure field} maps a realized failed contact state to
the minimal contact edit that would have crossed a mechanical success boundary.
The field is not a post hoc explanation of a classifier decision.  Its variables
are physical contact interventions: contact displacements, forces, normals, or
frictional patches that a robot controller could attempt to change.

The contribution is deliberately narrow.  We do not claim a new tactile sensor,
a larger model, a new benchmark, or a real-robot result.  Instead we isolate a
field assumption that is often hidden: a scalar grasp failure score is assumed to
be a sufficient object for repair.  We show this can be false even in a
two-contact quasi-static pinch.  The resulting counterexample is small enough to
analyze exactly and runnable enough to audit.

The paper makes three claims.  First, in a symmetric pinch model, the failure
field has a closed form as a minimum-norm projection onto a wrench-feasible
interval.  Second, any deterministic repair rule that factors a failed state
only through a scalar invariant of absolute failure magnitude cannot choose the
correct repair for paired states with opposite wrench-balance error.  Third, in
a 20,000-case simulation, this loss of repair direction matters operationally:
the field repairs all feasible failures in one step, while scalar-only
sign-guessing rules repair roughly half.

\section{Related Work and Novelty Boundary}

\paragraph{Grasp mechanics and quality.}
Analytic grasping defines stability through contact geometry, friction cones,
force closure, and wrench-space quality \citep{ferrari1992planning,
bicchi2000robotic,roa2015grasp}.  Large-scale grasp datasets and planners use
such criteria as labels or training targets for grasp synthesis
\citep{mahler2017dexnet}.  This paper uses mechanics as the success predicate,
not as the novelty.  The new object is the counterfactual contact edit from a
realized failed state back to the success set.

\paragraph{Tactile grasp stability and repair.}
Tactile sensors can measure slip, shear, contact shape, and grasp stability
\citep{yuan2015measurement,lambeta2020digit,chebotar2016bigs}.  Closed-loop
tactile methods can stabilize or regrasp objects using slip prediction,
simulated tactile transforms, or manipulation primitives
\citep{veiga2018grip,hogan2018tactile,hogan2020tactile}.  These works motivate
the field box: contact-rich robot manipulation with tactile evidence.  They do
not collapse the present claim because they generally treat tactile evidence as
input to a predictor or controller, whereas the proposed representation makes
the contact field itself the counterfactual variable.

\paragraph{Counterfactual explanations.}
Counterfactual explanation methods ask which input changes would flip a model
decision \citep{pearl2009causality,wachter2017counterfactual}.  Generic feature
or pixel edits are insufficient for grasp repair unless the edited variables are
executable contacts.  We therefore restrict counterfactuals to contact-space
interventions and evaluate them against a mechanical boundary.

\paragraph{Hostile boundary.}
The closest hostile interpretations are: ``this is just grasp quality with a
gradient'', ``this is tactile servoing'', and ``force closure already solves
it''.  In the analytic toy model, the true margin gradient can indeed recover
the same projection if it is available and calibrated.  The claim is more
specific: a scalar failure label or same-score representation is not itself an
adequate repair object.  A failure field records the executable contact edit,
including its sign and distribution across contacts.

\section{Counterfactual Failure Fields}

Let $x$ be a realized contact state and let $\mathcal{S}$ be the set of contact
states satisfying a mechanical success predicate.  A counterfactual failure
field is the solution of
\begin{equation}
  F(x) \in \arg\min_{\delta \in \mathcal{A}(x)}
  c(\delta)
  \quad \textrm{s.t.} \quad x+\delta \in \mathcal{S},
  \label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}(x)$ is the set of physically executable contact edits and
$c$ is a movement, force, or risk cost.  If the set is non-unique, the honest
object is a repair set; in this paper we use the minimum Euclidean displacement.

\subsection{A Planar Pinch Model}

Consider two opposing point contacts on a planar object.  The left contact is at
$(-a,y_L)$ with force $(N,t_L)$ and the right contact is at $(a,y_R)$ with force
$(-N,t_R)$, where $N>0$ is the normal force and $|t_i|\leq \mu N$ are tangential
friction forces.  The grasp must resist a vertical load $W$ and external torque
$\tau$.  Quasi-static balance requires
\begin{equation}
  t_L + t_R = W,
  \quad
  -a t_L + a t_R + N(y_R-y_L) = \tau .
\end{equation}
Writing $d=t_R-t_L$, feasibility is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  |d| \leq 2\mu N - W,
  \quad
  d = \frac{\tau - N(y_R-y_L)}{a}.
\end{equation}
Therefore the contact state succeeds exactly when
\begin{equation}
  |e| \leq h,
  \quad
  e = \frac{\tau}{N} - (y_R-y_L),
  \quad
  h = \frac{a(2\mu N-W)}{N}.
  \label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
Here $e$ is the wrench-balance error expressed as a missing contact-height
difference, and $h$ is the radius of the feasible interval.

For a failed state $|e|>h$, the minimum-norm displacement
$\delta=(\delta_L,\delta_R)$ that reaches the closest boundary of
Equation~\ref{eq:interval} is
\begin{equation}
  g = e - \operatorname{sign}(e)h,
  \quad
  \delta_R = \frac{g}{2},
  \quad
  \delta_L = -\frac{g}{2}.
  \label{eq:closedform}
\end{equation}
The field says whether the right contact must move up relative to the left or
down relative to the left.  A scalar score based only on $|e|$ preserves failure
magnitude but erases this sign.

\begin{proposition}[Scalar insufficiency for deterministic repair]
Fix any $r>h$ and two failed states $x_+$ and $x_-$ with errors $e(x_+)=r$ and
$e(x_-)=-r$.  Let $\phi(x)=\psi(|e(x)|)$ be any scalar representation that is
identical for equal absolute error.  Any deterministic repair rule
$\pi(\phi(x))$ assigns the same contact edit to $x_+$ and $x_-$.  The
minimum-norm stabilizing fields for the two states have opposite
$\delta_R-\delta_L$ signs.  Thus $\pi$ cannot equal the minimum stabilizing
field on both states, and cannot deterministically repair both by choosing the
correct signed minimum edit.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textbf{Proof.}
Since $|e(x_+)|=|e(x_-)|=r$, $\phi(x_+)=\phi(x_-)$, so the deterministic rule
returns the same edit for both.  From Equation~\ref{eq:closedform}, the required
change in contact-height difference is $r-h$ for $x_+$ and $-(r-h)$ for $x_-$.
These have opposite signs.  A single signed edit cannot be the minimum field for
both states. \hfill $\square$

\section{Runnable Evidence}

The experiment samples failed but repairable contact states from the model
above.  For each state, it computes the analytic field in
Equation~\ref{eq:closedform}.  We compare against scalar-only rules that know
the required failure magnitude $|e|-h$ but do not know the sign.  One baseline
guesses a random sign; another always guesses the majority sign in the sampled
set.  These baselines are intentionally favorable to scalar information because
they know how far the state is from the boundary.  They fail only because the
scalar representation loses repair direction.

Figure~\ref{fig:results} and Table~\ref{tab:results} show the operational
effect of scalarization.  The generated paired examples include equal failure
scores of 0.78245 with contact-difference edits $+0.08$ and $-0.08$, and equal
failure scores of 0.94685 with edits $+0.18$ and $-0.18$.  Across the random
sample, the repair sign is nearly balanced, so scalar-only repair leaves almost
one full bit of unresolved action information.  The analytic field, by
construction, supplies that missing bit and reaches the nearest success boundary
in one step whenever the repaired contacts remain inside the finger travel
limits.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
The result supports a representational claim: for contact-rich grasp repair,
there exist mechanically simple settings where a scalar failure score is not a
sufficient repair object.  The minimum counterfactual contact field retains
which contact relation must change and how far it must move.

\paragraph{What it does not support.}
The experiment is not a real-robot demonstration.  It does not model compliant
contact patches, object pose uncertainty, rolling contacts, multi-finger
hands, tactile calibration, or high-speed slip dynamics.  It also does not show
that learned tactile policies cannot implicitly learn useful repairs.  A
differentiable analytic margin with access to contact coordinates can produce
the same projection in this toy case.  The point is that the projection should
be the object represented and evaluated, rather than hidden behind a terminal
label.

\paragraph{Generalization path.}
In richer settings, $\mathcal{A}(x)$ can include contact patch translations,
normal-force redistributions, tangential traction changes, or frictional
surface edits.  Equation~\ref{eq:field} then becomes a constrained optimization
or an amortized prediction target.  Non-unique fields should be reported as
sets, not collapsed prematurely.  This makes ambiguity visible to a controller
instead of forcing all failure states through one scalar.

\section{Conclusion}

Counterfactual grasp failure fields change the modeled object from ``a grasp
failed'' to ``this contact edit would have made the realized grasp succeed.''
The mechanism is old mechanics used in a different place: not only to score a
candidate grasp, but to represent a failed contact state as a minimal physical
repair.  The 2D pinch model is small, but it exposes a real assumption.  If two
failures have the same score but opposite repairs, scalar failure prediction is
not enough.  Contact-indexed counterfactual fields offer a more direct target
for tactile grasp recovery.

\section{Additional Derivation}

The friction constraints imply
$|W+d|\leq 2\mu N$ and $|W-d|\leq 2\mu N$, where $d=t_R-t_L$.  For $W\geq 0$,
their intersection is $W-2\mu N \leq d \leq 2\mu N-W$.  If $W<2\mu N$, this is
the interval $|d|\leq 2\mu N-W$.  Substituting the moment-balance expression for
$d$ gives Equation~\ref{eq:interval}.  The Euclidean projection follows because
the feasible set depends on $\delta$ only through
$\delta_R-\delta_L$; the minimum norm allocation of a required difference change
$g$ is the equal split $(\delta_L,\delta_R)=(-g/2,g/2)$.
